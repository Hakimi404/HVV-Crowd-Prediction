Project Overview

How Hamburg Moves is a data-driven narrative about mobility in Hamburg — a city whose movement patterns reflect its culture, weather, and events. Using real data from HVV transport systems, cycling sensors, event calendars, and environmental trackers, we analyzed how the flow of people changes throughout the day and week.

Over four weeks, we collected millions of records and built predictive models to understand how and why mobility fluctuates. The goal: to transform data into a story — where every chart represents a piece of the city’s heartbeat.

The Visual Story

1. Predicting Crowds — Station Altona

This chart compares real passenger counts with predictions from XGBoost and Random Forest models. The models were trained on several features — station, weekday, hour, minute, and event presence — to forecast passenger volumes. The training period was from mid-November to December 23, and the test period covered December 24–31.

The blue and orange curves nearly overlap, proving that the models capture Hamburg’s mobility rhythm with impressive accuracy. Even during the chaotic pre-Christmas week, the predictions follow the real-world peaks and drops with only minor deviations. This proves that short-term forecasting (minutes to hours ahead) is extremely reliable for transport optimization.

2. When Events Change Everything

The following visualization separates weekday activity into two scenarios: days with events and days without events. Each point shows the average movement over all 5-minute intervals across stations.

When events are present (blue line), we see a dramatic surge around 16:00–18:00, far exceeding the normal commuter peaks (orange line). This effect is strongest near venues like Barclaycard Arena and Volksparkstadion, where major concerts or football games take place. The data highlights how events can temporarily reshape mobility patterns across the entire city network.

3. The City’s Heartbeat — Hamburg Heatmap

This heatmap shows Hamburg’s movement density at 12:00 on December 8. Each bubble represents a station; its size indicates the total sensor detections within a 1 km radius, and its color intensity reflects crowd density.

The bright yellow circles around Jungfernstieg, Hauptbahnhof, and Sternschanze reveal where the city pulses strongest. In contrast, peripheral stations like Harburg and Bergedorf remain cooler (purple), showing local calm. Together, they illustrate how Hamburg’s urban core drives daily flow while outer zones breathe slower.

4. Where Time Slows Down — Delay by Station

This comparison analyzes the average delay per station across transport types: U-Bahn, S-Bahn, Regional trains, and Buses. Delay is measured as the mean seconds of lateness aggregated per 5-minute interval.

Predictably, Hauptbahnhof dominates with the highest delay levels across all modes — a reflection of its central role and complexity. Meanwhile, Jungfernstieg and Sternschanze show moderate, consistent delays due to interchange traffic. This visualization highlights structural bottlenecks where congestion and scheduling conflicts accumulate.

5. Why Trains Run Late — Delay by Cause

These heatmaps break down delay causes for S-Bahn and Regional trains across time of day. Darker shades indicate higher average delay durations.

Suburban lines are most affected by police investigations and medical incidents in the morning, while regional trains experience delays from signal issues and track obstructions in the afternoon. The analysis reveals daily operational stress points — valuable input for maintenance scheduling and incident management.

6. Forecasting Accuracy — The Time Challenge

These plots evaluate prediction accuracy across different forecast horizons (5 minutes, 15 minutes, 1 hour). For short-term intervals, the models perform with near-perfect precision — a 5-minute forecast misses on average by only 1.23 people per interval.

As horizons grow longer, MAE rises slowly while RMSE increases sharply, showing that longer forecasts accumulate uncertainty. The bottom line: Random Forests are excellent for real-time forecasting but less reliable for long-term traffic planning due to growing unpredictability from weather, human behavior, and events.

7. Peaks and Events — How Culture Moves the City

Finally, we analyze how many peak activity moments coincide with major events. The first bar chart shows the percentage of detected peaks that overlap with event times (±30 minutes). Stations like Bergedorf and Wandsbek Markt show over 70–80% of peaks related to events.

The second chart compares the probability of peaks with and without events. It clearly shows that event days significantly increase the likelihood of strong mobility surges — reinforcing the link between social life and transport demand.

Insights & Reflections

From raw data to living narrative, this project uncovered how Hamburg breathes. Events, weather, and time shape its rhythm — one that repeats daily yet never exactly the same.

Our findings highlight the potential of real-time analytics for public transport optimization, crowd control, and urban planning. Predicting movement isn’t just technical — it’s human. Every peak, every delay, every surge tells a story about how cities live.
